\begin{remark*}[Exposition]
Completeness is often used before the full language of sequence theory has
been developed.  This chapter therefore records several
\emph{process-level completeness consequences}: arguments that are shaped like
sequences but are used as controlled approximation procedures.
\end{remark*}

\begin{remark*}[Process-level completeness consequences]
The following consequences are bridges from the least-upper-bound axiom to the
later sequence chapter.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A bounded increasing process has a limiting endpoint, namely the
  supremum of the values already produced.
  \item A bounded decreasing process has a limiting endpoint, namely the
  infimum of the values already produced.
  \item A shrinking nested closed interval process determines a unique real
  point when the lengths shrink to zero.
  \item The Archimedean reciprocal process $1/n$ tends to zero in the
  epsilon sense used by approximation arguments.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark*}

\begin{remark*}[Boundary with the Sequences chapter]
These statements deliberately do not replace the full machinery of sequences.
The mature vocabulary of convergence, Cauchy sequences, subsequences,
Bolzano--Weierstrass, and the systematic algebra of limits belongs to
\hyperref[chap:sequences]{Sequences}.  Here the goal is narrower: completeness
guarantees that monotone or nested approximation processes do not aim at a
missing real number.
\end{remark*}
